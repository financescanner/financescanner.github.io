How to read a candlestick chart

Each candle summarises one time period — a minute, an hour, a day or a month, depending on the timeframe you choose. The thick body spans the open and close price, and the thin wicks reach the highest and lowest prices traded in that period. On this chart a green (or hollow) body means the price closed above its open; a red (or filled) body means it closed lower.

Common single-candle signals

  • Doji — open and close almost equal; shows indecision between buyers and sellers.
  • Hammer — small body with a long lower wick after a fall; sellers pushed price down but buyers recovered it.
  • Shooting star — small body with a long upper wick after a rise; buyers failed to hold the highs.
  • Marubozu — a long body with almost no wicks; one side controlled the whole period.

Common multi-candle patterns

  • Bullish / bearish engulfing — a candle whose body fully covers the previous one, hinting at a possible change in direction.
  • Morning / evening star — a three-candle reversal pattern around a small middle candle.
  • Three white soldiers / three black crows — three strong candles in the same direction in a row.
  • Inside bar — a candle contained within the previous one; often precedes a breakout.

Using candlesticks with stocks

For shares and indices such as the Nifty 50 or Sensex, daily candles help you see the broader trend, while 5- to 60-minute candles show intraday behaviour. Volume bars under the chart show how much trading supported a move — a breakout on high volume is generally more meaningful than one on thin volume. Results depend on the market session: Indian equities trade roughly 9:15–15:30 IST, US stocks 9:30–16:00 New York time.

Using candlesticks with currency pairs

Forex trades around the clock on weekdays, so candles are continuous rather than interrupted by overnight gaps. Pairs such as USD/INR are quoted as how many units of the second currency one unit of the first buys, so a rising chart means the first currency is strengthening. Prices move in small increments called pips, and liquidity peaks when the London and New York sessions overlap.

Good habits

  • Check more than one timeframe before drawing conclusions.
  • Treat patterns as probabilities, not promises — confirm with trend, volume or support and resistance levels.
  • Decide your risk and stop level before you enter any trade; our risk / reward and position-sizing calculators can help.
